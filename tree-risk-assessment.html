Tree risk assessment

A defensible position on a tree you are worried about. Not a verdict pulled out of the air, but a stated methodology, a recorded reasoning chain, and a recommendation you can act on and later justify.

Why this one costs more

Because the thing you are buying is not the inspection. It is a document that has to survive being questioned, possibly years later, possibly by somebody with an interest in showing it was wrong.

That means the methodology is stated, each step of the reasoning is visible, and the conclusion is no stronger than the evidence supports. It takes longer than a condition report and it is priced accordingly.

The method

I hold the ISA Tree Risk Assessment Qualification. As far as I can find, no other consulting arborist advertising in metropolitan Melbourne publishes TRAQ or QTRA credentials, which is odd given how often these assessments decide whether a tree stands.

The assessment works through three things separately rather than collapsing them into a single impression:

  • Likelihood of failure. What could fail, how likely is it, over what timeframe
  • Likelihood of impacting a target. What is beneath it, how often, for how long
  • Consequences. What actually happens if it does

Those combine into a risk rating with the working shown at each step. A rating without the working is an opinion with a number attached to it.

What is in the report

  • Tree identification, dimensions and condition
  • Site context and target occupancy, assessed rather than assumed
  • Defects described specifically, with photographs
  • Likelihood of failure and of impact, stated separately
  • Consequences and the resulting risk rating
  • Mitigation options with the residual risk after each, so removal becomes a choice rather than a default
  • A recommended reinspection interval
  • The matrix used, reproduced, so the rating can be checked against the method

Removal is not the only answer

A sound risk assessment usually finds that pruning, managing the target or shortening the inspection interval brings the risk to an acceptable level. If the answer genuinely is removal I will say so plainly, and I have no commercial reason to reach that conclusion because I do not do the removal.

Where a council is involved

If the tree is protected by an overlay or a local law, the risk assessment is usually what supports the removal application. Councils are rightly sceptical of reports that find a convenient hazard, and a council arborist will generally spot one. Stating the method and showing the reasoning is what carries the argument.

Bayside is worth noting here: it requires a minimum AQF Level 5 arborist who is also formally trained in a tree risk assessment method. It is the only council in Melbourne I have found that sets that bar explicitly.

Common questions

What does a tree risk assessment cost?

More than a condition report, because the deliverable has to survive being questioned later. It is quoted per tree and per site, fixed in writing before I start.

What is TRAQ?

The Tree Risk Assessment Qualification, issued by the International Society of Arboriculture. It sets a structured method for assessing likelihood of failure, likelihood of impact and consequences, so a risk rating can be explained rather than merely asserted.

Will a risk assessment get me a removal permit?

It will if the risk genuinely warrants removal and the reasoning is sound. It will not manufacture a case that is not there, and attempting that tends to damage the application rather than help it.

How often should trees be reassessed?

It depends on the tree, the defects found and what sits beneath it. The report gives you an interval rather than leaving that open.
