Tree risk assessment

Why a risk assessment takes longer than a condition report

You are not really paying for the inspection. You are paying for a document that has to hold up when somebody questions it, which might be years later and might be somebody with a reason to want it wrong.

So the report names the method, shows the reasoning at each step rather than jumping to a rating, and stops short of a conclusion the evidence will not carry. It takes longer to write than a condition report and it costs more.

The method

Tree Risk Assessment Qualification is one of the two structured risk methods in common use here, the other being QTRA, the Quantified Tree Risk Assessment system. When I last looked, very few consulting arborists advertising in metropolitan Melbourne published a risk qualification of either kind on their website.

The assessment works through three things separately rather than collapsing them into a single impression:

  • Likelihood of failure. What could fail, how likely is it, over what timeframe
  • Likelihood of impacting a target. What is beneath it, how often, for how long
  • Consequences. What happens if it does, and to what

Those three combine into a risk rating, with the working shown at each step so you can see how it was reached.

What is in the report

  • Tree identification, dimensions and condition
  • Site context and target occupancy, assessed rather than assumed
  • Defects described specifically, with photographs
  • Likelihood of failure and of impact, stated separately
  • Consequences and the resulting risk rating
  • Mitigation options with the residual risk after each, so removal becomes a choice rather than a default
  • A recommended reinspection interval
  • The matrix used, reproduced, so the rating can be checked against the method

Removal is not the only answer

In most of the risk assessments I do, the answer turns out to be pruning, fencing off what sits underneath, or coming back sooner to look again. Where removal really is the answer I will say so, and I have no reason to reach for it because I do not do removals.

Where a council is involved

If the tree is protected by an overlay or a local law, the risk assessment is usually what supports the removal application. Council arborists see plenty of reports that find a convenient hazard and they are sceptical for good reason. I set out the method I used and show the reasoning behind each rating so the assessor can check it rather than take my word for it.

Bayside is worth noting here: it requires a minimum AQF Level 5 arborist who is also formally trained in a tree risk assessment method. It is the only council in Melbourne I have found that sets that bar explicitly.

Common questions

What does a tree risk assessment cost?

More than a condition report, because the deliverable has to survive being questioned later. It is quoted per tree and per site, fixed in writing before I start.

What is TRAQ?

It stands for Tree Risk Assessment Qualification and it is issued by the International Society of Arboriculture. It sets a structured method for assessing likelihood of failure, likelihood of impact and consequences, so a risk rating can be explained rather than merely asserted.

Will a risk assessment get me a removal permit?

It gives council the evidence to grant one where the risk genuinely warrants removal, though the decision is still theirs. If the evidence does not, I will tell you that before I write anything, and we can look at pruning or reducing what sits under the tree instead.

How often should trees be reassessed?

It depends on the tree, the defects found and what sits beneath it. The report gives you an interval rather than leaving that open.
